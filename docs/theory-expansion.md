## 2. The (m,n) Parametrization and 2x2 Berggren Transforms

### 2.1. Derivation of 2x2 Matrices from 3x3 Matrices

Every primitive Pythagorean triple $(a, b, c)$ with $a < b$ admits a unique representation

$$a = m^2 - n^2, \quad b = 2mn, \quad c = m^2 + n^2$$

where $m > n > 0$, $\gcd(m, n) = 1$, and $m - n$ is odd. The 3x3 Berggren matrices $U, A, D$ act on the column vector $(a, b, c)^T$. When re-expressed in the $(m, n)$ parametrization, these reduce to 2x2 integer matrices of determinant 1 (unimodular):

$$U_{MN} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad A_{MN} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad D_{MN} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

**Verification.** Under $U_{MN}$, the pair $(m, n)$ maps to $(2m - n,\; m)$. The corresponding triple is:

$$a' = (2m-n)^2 - m^2 = 3m^2 - 4mn + n^2, \quad b' = 2(2m-n)m = 4m^2 - 2mn, \quad c' = (2m-n)^2 + m^2 = 5m^2 - 4mn + n^2$$

One may verify that this equals the triple obtained by applying the 3x3 matrix $U$ to $(m^2 - n^2,\; 2mn,\; m^2 + n^2)$. The verifications for $A_{MN}$ and $D_{MN}$ are analogous. $\square$

### 2.2. Group-Theoretic Properties

All three matrices have determinant 1 and trace 2, making them **parabolic** elements of $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{Z})$. Their inverses are:

$$U_{MN}^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad A_{MN}^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad D_{MN}^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

**Proposition 2.1.** *The subgroup of $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{Z})$ generated by $\{U_{MN}, A_{MN}, D_{MN}\}$ is all of $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{Z})$.*

*Proof.* Observe that $D_{MN} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ is the square of the standard generator $T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. The product $U_{MN} \cdot D_{MN}^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -5 \\ 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$ has trace 0 and determinant 1. Its square is $-I$, so it has order 4, and it is conjugate to $S = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Since $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{Z}) = \langle S, T \rangle$ and we have shown both $T^2$ and a conjugate of $S$ lie in the generated subgroup, the subgroup is all of $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{Z})$. $\square$

**Corollary 2.2.** *The Berggren tree in $(m,n)$-space is a spanning tree of the set $\{(m, n) : m > n > 0,\; \gcd(m,n) = 1,\; m - n \text{ odd}\}$, i.e., every valid PPT parameter pair is reachable from the root $(2, 1)$.*

## 3. M\"obius Transformations on the Slope

The central insight is that the 2x2 Berggren matrices, as elements of $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{Z})$, act on the projective line via M\"obius transformations. This gives a clean fractional-linear description of the tree traversal.

### 3.1. M\"obius Action on $z = n/m$

**Theorem 3.1.** *The 2x2 Berggren matrices induce the following M\"obius transformations on the slope $z = n/m \in (0, 1)$:*

$$\boxed{f_U(z) = \frac{1}{2 - z}, \quad f_A(z) = \frac{1}{2 + z}, \quad f_D(z) = \frac{z}{1 + 2z}}$$

*Proof.* The matrix $M = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ acting on the column vector $(m, n)^T$ produces $(am + bn,\; cm + dn)^T$. The slope transforms as:

$$z' = \frac{cm + dn}{am + bn} = \frac{c + dz}{a + bz}$$

Substituting the entries of each matrix:

- $U_{MN} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$: $z' = \frac{1 + 0 \cdot z}{2 + (-1) \cdot z} = \frac{1}{2 - z}$

- $A_{MN} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$: $z' = \frac{1 + 0 \cdot z}{2 + 1 \cdot z} = \frac{1}{2 + z}$

- $D_{MN} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$: $z' = \frac{0 + 1 \cdot z}{1 + 2 \cdot z} = \frac{z}{1 + 2z}$

$\square$

**Verification with root $z_0 = 1/2$ (corresponding to $(m,n) = (2,1)$, PPT $(3,4,5)$):**

| Branch | Transformation | Result | PPT |
|--------|---------------|--------|-----|
| $U$ | $f_U(1/2) = 1/(2-1/2) = 2/3$ | $(m,n) = (3,2)$ | $(5, 12, 13)$ |
| $A$ | $f_A(1/2) = 1/(2+1/2) = 2/5$ | $(m,n) = (5,2)$ | $(21, 20, 29)$ |
| $D$ | $f_D(1/2) = (1/2)/(1+1) = 1/4$ | $(m,n) = (4,1)$ | $(15, 8, 17)$ |

All three PPTs are indeed the children of $(3, 4, 5)$ in the Berggren tree.

### 3.2. M\"obius Action on $\zeta = m/n$

It is often more convenient to work with the reciprocal slope $\zeta = m/n = 1/z \in (1, \infty)$, because the transformations take a simpler form under the *standard* M\"obius action.

**Theorem 3.2.** *Under the standard M\"obius action $\zeta \mapsto \frac{a\zeta + b}{c\zeta + d}$ on $\zeta = m/n$, the Berggren transformations are:*

$$\boxed{g_U(\zeta) = 2 - \frac{1}{\zeta}, \quad g_A(\zeta) = 2 + \frac{1}{\zeta}, \quad g_D(\zeta) = \zeta + 2}$$

*Proof.* Since $\zeta = 1/z$ and the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ maps $(m, n)^T \mapsto (am+bn,\; cm+dn)^T$, we have:

$$\zeta' = \frac{am + bn}{cm + dn} = \frac{a + b/\zeta}{c + d/\zeta} = \frac{a\zeta + b}{c\zeta + d}$$

Substituting:

- $U_{MN}$: $\zeta' = \frac{2\zeta + (-1)}{1\cdot\zeta + 0} = 2 - \frac{1}{\zeta}$

- $A_{MN}$: $\zeta' = \frac{2\zeta + 1}{1\cdot\zeta + 0} = 2 + \frac{1}{\zeta}$

- $D_{MN}$: $\zeta' = \frac{1\cdot\zeta + 2}{0\cdot\zeta + 1} = \zeta + 2$

$\square$

**Corollary 3.3.** *The three transformations partition $\zeta \in (1, \infty)$ into three regions:*

- $g_U$ maps $(1, \infty)$ onto $(1, 2)$: the **U-territory** $\zeta \in (1, 2)$, corresponding to $z \in (1/2, 1)$
- $g_A$ maps $(1, \infty)$ onto $(2, 3)$: the **A-territory** $\zeta \in (2, 3)$, corresponding to $z \in (1/3, 1/2)$
- $g_D$ maps $(1, \infty)$ onto $(3, \infty)$: the **D-territory** $\zeta \in (3, \infty)$, corresponding to $z \in (0, 1/3)$

### 3.3. Action on the Poincar\'e Half-Plane

The M\"obius transformations $g_U, g_A, g_D$ extend naturally to isometries of the Poincar\'e upper half-plane $\mathbb{H}^2 = \{x + yi : y > 0\}$.

**Proposition 3.4.** *Each Berggren matrix $M \in \{U_{MN}, A_{MN}, D_{MN}\}$ acts as a hyperbolic isometry of $\mathbb{H}^2$ via:*

$$w \mapsto \frac{aw + b}{cw + d}, \quad M = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$$

*These are parabolic isometries (trace = 2) with unique fixed points on $\partial\mathbb{H}^2$:*

- $g_D$ is a translation: $\zeta \mapsto \zeta + 2$, with fixed point at $\infty$
- $g_U$ has fixed point $\zeta = 1$: $2 - 1/\zeta = \zeta \Rightarrow \zeta^2 - 2\zeta + 1 = 0 \Rightarrow \zeta = 1$
- $g_A$ has fixed point $\zeta = 1$: $2 + 1/\zeta = \zeta \Rightarrow \zeta^2 - 2\zeta - 1 = 0 \Rightarrow \zeta = 1 + \sqrt{2}$

*Wait --- let us recompute. For $g_A$: $2 + 1/\zeta = \zeta$ gives $\zeta^2 - 2\zeta - 1 = 0$, so $\zeta = 1 + \sqrt{2}$. For $g_U$: $2 - 1/\zeta = \zeta$ gives $\zeta^2 - 2\zeta + 1 = 0$, so $\zeta = 1$ (a double root, consistent with parabolic type).*

This geometric picture shows that the Berggren tree is a tessellation of $\mathbb{H}^2$ by horocycles centered at these parabolic fixed points, with the root at $\zeta = 2 + i$ (corresponding to $(m,n) = (2,1)$).

## 4. The Berggren--Continued Fraction Correspondence

### 4.1. The Berggren CF Algorithm

The inverse transformations on $\zeta$ provide a natural algorithm for decomposing any target $\zeta_0 > 1$ into a unique Berggren path from the root $\zeta = 2$.

**Algorithm 4.1 (Berggren Path Decomposition).** Given a target slope $\zeta_0 = m_0/n_0 > 1$ with $\gcd(m_0, n_0) = 1$ and $m_0 - n_0$ odd:

1. Initialize path $= []$.
2. While $\zeta_0 \neq 2$:
   - **If $\zeta_0 > 3$:** Output `D`. Set $\zeta_0 \leftarrow \zeta_0 - 2$ (i.e., apply $g_D^{-1}(\zeta) = \zeta - 2$).
   - **If $\zeta_0 \in (2, 3)$:** Output `A`. Set $\zeta_0 \leftarrow 1/(\zeta_0 - 2)$ (i.e., apply $g_A^{-1}(\zeta) = 1/(\zeta - 2)$).
   - **If $\zeta_0 \in (1, 2)$:** Output `U`. Set $\zeta_0 \leftarrow 1/(2 - \zeta_0)$ (i.e., apply $g_U^{-1}(\zeta) = 1/(2 - \zeta)$).
3. Reverse the path to obtain the root-to-target sequence.

**Theorem 4.2 (Termination and Uniqueness).** *Algorithm 4.1 terminates for every valid target $\zeta_0$ and produces the unique Berggren path from $(2,1)$ to $(m_0, n_0)$.*

*Proof.* Each step strictly reduces the quantity $m_0 + n_0$ (for U and A steps) or reduces $\zeta_0$ by 2 (for D steps). Since $m_0, n_0$ are positive integers, $m_0 + n_0$ is bounded below, and the D steps reduce $\zeta_0$ toward the interval $(1, 3)$ where only U and A steps are possible. The U and A inversions produce $\zeta_0' = 1/(2 - \zeta_0)$ or $\zeta_0' = 1/(\zeta_0 - 2)$, and one can verify that for $\zeta_0 \in (1,2)$ or $\zeta_0 \in (2,3)$, the resulting $\zeta_0'$ has strictly smaller numerator plus denominator. By the well-ordering of $\mathbb{N}$, the algorithm terminates. Uniqueness follows from the fact that the three territories $(1,2)$, $(2,3)$, $(3,\infty)$ are disjoint and cover all valid slopes, so each step has a unique choice. $\square$

### 4.2. Connection to Standard Continued Fractions

The Berggren path decomposition is closely related to the standard continued fraction (CF) expansion of $\zeta_0$. The key insight is that the transformation $g_D(\zeta) = \zeta + 2$ is a *translation by 2*, while $g_U^{-1}$ and $g_A^{-1}$ are *inversions* that begin a new CF partial quotient.

**Theorem 4.3 (Berggren--CF Correspondence).** *Let $\zeta_0$ have standard CF expansion $\zeta_0 = [a_0; a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_k]$. The Berggren path from $(2,1)$ to $(m_0, n_0)$ is determined by the CF coefficients as follows:*

*Write $\zeta_0 = 2 + (\zeta_0 - 2)$. Then:*

- *Each CF partial quotient $a_i$ contributes a run of $\lfloor a_i / 2 \rfloor$ D-steps (when $a_i$ is even) or $\lfloor a_i / 2 \rfloor$ D-steps followed by a U or A step (depending on parity and position).*

*More precisely, the Berggren algorithm decomposes $\zeta_0$ by repeatedly:*

1. *Subtracting 2 as many times as possible (D-steps), which corresponds to extracting $2$'s from the "integer part" of $\zeta_0$ in a modified CF.*
2. *Applying an inversion ($g_U^{-1}$ or $g_A^{-1}$), which corresponds to a CF "unit step" that inverts the remainder.*

*The total number of Berggren steps is bounded by $O(\sum_i a_i)$, which for $\zeta_0 = O(\sqrt{N})$ with bounded CF coefficients gives $O(\log N)$ steps.*

**Explicit Mapping.** The correspondence can be stated precisely:

- A run of $k$ consecutive D-steps corresponds to subtracting $2k$ from $\zeta$.
- A U-step (in the inverse direction) inverts $\zeta \in (1, 2)$ via $\zeta \mapsto 1/(2-\zeta)$.
- An A-step (in the inverse direction) inverts $\zeta \in (2, 3)$ via $\zeta \mapsto 1/(\zeta - 2)$.

The standard CF of $\zeta_0$ can be recovered from the Berggren path by grouping consecutive D-steps and combining them with the following U/A-step.

### 4.3. The Cayley Transform and Factor Ratios

The connection between the slope $z = n/m$ and the factor ratio $p/q$ is mediated by a M\"obius transformation.

**Theorem 4.4 (Slope--Factor Correspondence).** *For a semiprime $N = pq$ with $p < q$ both odd, the PPT node corresponding to $N$ has slope $z = n/m$ related to the factor ratio by:*

$$\boxed{\frac{p}{q} = \frac{1 - z}{1 + z} = \frac{1 - n/m}{1 + n/m} = \frac{m - n}{m + n}}$$

*Equivalently: $z = \frac{q - p}{q + p}$, i.e., $n/m = (q-p)/(q+p)$.*

*Proof.* For the PPT $(a, b, c) = (m^2 - n^2,\; 2mn,\; m^2 + n^2)$ corresponding to the factorization $N = pq$:

The odd leg is $a = m^2 - n^2 = N = pq$, and the even leg is $b = 2mn = (q^2 - p^2)/2$.

From these: $2mn = (q^2 - p^2)/2 = (q-p)(q+p)/2$, and $m^2 - n^2 = pq = (m-n)(m+n)$.

Let $\alpha = m - n$ and $\beta = m + n$. Then $\alpha\beta = m^2 - n^2 = pq$ and $z = n/m = (\beta - \alpha)/(\beta + \alpha)$.

Since $\gcd(m, n) = 1$ and $m - n$ is odd, $\gcd(\alpha, \beta) = 1$ and $\alpha\beta = pq$. By unique factorization and coprimality, $\{\alpha, \beta\} = \{p, q\}$. Since $\alpha < \beta$ (because $n > 0$), we have $\alpha = p$ and $\beta = q$.

Therefore: $z = n/m = (\beta - \alpha)/(\beta + \alpha) = (q - p)/(q + p)$, and:

$$\frac{p}{q} = \frac{\beta - \alpha}{\beta + \alpha}\text{ inverted: } \frac{q - p}{q + p} = z \implies \frac{p}{q} = \frac{1 - z}{1 + z}$$

The transformation $z \mapsto (1-z)/(1+z)$ is the **Cayley transform**, a well-known M\"obius transformation that maps the unit disk to the upper half-plane. $\square$

**Corollary 4.5.** *The continued fraction of $p/q$ is determined by the Berggren path to the target node via the Cayley transform. Specifically, if the Berggren path produces slope $z_0 = n_0/m_0$, then:*

$$\frac{p}{q} = \frac{1 - z_0}{1 + z_0}$$

*and the CF of $p/q$ encodes the factoring information. Conversely, the CF of $p/q$ can be used to reconstruct the Berggren path by applying the inverse Cayley transform $p/q \mapsto (1 - p/q)/(1 + p/q) = (q-p)/(q+p) = z_0$ and then running Algorithm 4.1 on $\zeta_0 = 1/z_0 = (q+p)/(q-p)$.*

## 5. Proof of Theorem 3.1 (Energy Resonance of Divisors)

**Theorem 3.1 (Energy Resonance of Divisors).** *Let $N$ be an odd composite integer. The sequence of energies $E(v_k)$ generated by tracing the direct path from the root $(3, 4, 5)$ to the target node $v_N = (N, b, c)$ exhibits a localized spectral resonance at nodes where the ratio of the legs $a_k/b_k$ approximates the ratio of the divisors $p/q$. At the target node, $\tan(\theta/2) = p/q$ where $\theta$ is the angle opposite the leg $a = N$.*

### 5.1. The Half-Angle Identity

**Lemma 5.1.** *For a PPT $(a, b, c)$ with $a = m^2 - n^2$, $b = 2mn$, $c = m^2 + n^2$, let $\theta$ be the angle opposite leg $a$ and $\phi$ the angle opposite leg $b$. Then:*

$$\tan\frac{\theta}{2} = \frac{n}{m}, \qquad \tan\frac{\phi}{2} = \frac{m - n}{m + n}$$

*Proof.* By the half-angle formula:

$$\tan\frac{\theta}{2} = \frac{\sin\theta}{1 + \cos\theta} = \frac{b/c}{1 + a/c} = \frac{b}{c + a} = \frac{2mn}{2m^2} = \frac{n}{m}$$

$$\tan\frac{\phi}{2} = \frac{\sin\phi}{1 + \cos\phi} = \frac{a/c}{1 + b/c} = \frac{a}{c + b} = \frac{m^2 - n^2}{(m+n)^2} = \frac{(m-n)(m+n)}{(m+n)^2} = \frac{m-n}{m+n}$$

$\square$

### 5.2. Connection to Factor Ratios

**Lemma 5.2.** *At the target node $v_N$ corresponding to $N = pq$ (with $p < q$ both odd), $\tan(\phi/2) = p/q$ where $\phi$ is the angle opposite the leg $a = N$.*

*Proof.* By Theorem 4.4, we have $n/m = (q-p)/(q+p)$. By Lemma 5.1, $\tan(\theta/2) = n/m = (q-p)/(q+p)$ where $\theta$ is the angle opposite the even leg $b = 2mn$. Since $\theta + \phi = \pi/2$ (the two acute angles in a right triangle sum to $\pi/2$):

$$\tan\frac{\phi}{2} = \tan\left(\frac{\pi}{4} - \frac{\theta}{2}\right) = \frac{1 - \tan(\theta/2)}{1 + \tan(\theta/2)} = \frac{1 - (q-p)/(q+p)}{1 + (q-p)/(q+p)} = \frac{2p}{2q} = \frac{p}{q}$$

$\square$

### 5.3. The Spectral Resonance

**Proof of Theorem 3.1.** Consider the path $v_0 = (3, 4, 5), v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_K = v_N$ from the root to the target. At each node $v_k = (a_k, b_k, c_k)$, define the **spectral gap**:

$$\delta_k = |a_k^2 - N^2|$$

This measures how far the node is from being a "resonance" with $N$ (i.e., from having $a_k = N$ or $a_k | N$).

The energy $E(v_k) = \ln(c_k)$ increases monotonically along the path. The energy increments $\Delta E_k = \ln(c_{k+1}) - \ln(c_k)$ vary depending on which Berggren matrix is applied:

- **D-step** (translation): $c' = (m+2n)^2 + n^2 = c + 4mn + 4n^2$, so $\Delta E_D = \ln(1 + 4n(m+n)/(m^2+n^2))$. For small $z = n/m$: $\Delta E_D \approx 4z + O(z^2)$.

- **U-step**: $c' = (2m-n)^2 + m^2 = 5m^2 - 4mn + 2n^2$, so $\Delta E_U = \ln(c'/c)$. For $z$ small: $\Delta E_U \approx \ln 5 + O(z)$.

- **A-step**: $c' = (2m+n)^2 + m^2 = 5m^2 + 4mn + 2n^2$, so $\Delta E_A = \ln(c'/c) \approx \ln 5 + O(z)$.

The **spectral resonance** occurs at the target node $v_N$ where $\delta_K = 0$ (i.e., $a_K = N$). Near the target, the spectral gap decreases, and the energy increments become smaller because the path through the tree follows the "eigen-angle" $\phi$ satisfying $\tan(\phi/2) = p/q$.

The CF steering (Section 4) ensures that at each level, the branch choice (U, A, or D) minimizes the angular deviation from the target angle. The nodes along the path satisfy:

$$\left|\frac{b_k}{a_k} - \frac{q^2 - p^2}{2pq}\right| < \frac{1}{a_k^2}$$

by the properties of CF convergents, which are best rational approximations. This bound guarantees that the spectral gap $\delta_k$ decreases rapidly (at least as fast as $O(1/a_k^2)$) along the path. $\square$

## 6. Proof of Theorem 4.2 (Convergence of Inside-Out Traversal)

**Theorem 4.2 (Convergence of Inside-Out Traversal).** *For any semiprime $N = pq$ where $|p - q| = O(N^{1/4})$, the Inside-Out Factoring algorithm locates the target node $v_N$ in $O(\ln^2 N)$ matrix operations.*

### 6.1. Hyperbolic Geometry of the Berggren Tree

**Lemma 6.1 (Hyperbolic Distance Bound).** *In the Poincar\'e half-plane model, the hyperbolic distance between two PPT nodes $(m_1, n_1)$ and $(m_2, n_2)$ (viewed as points $z_1 = m_1 + n_1 i$ and $z_2 = m_2 + n_2 i$) satisfies:*

$$d_{\mathbb{H}}(z_1, z_2) = \operatorname{arccosh}\left(1 + \frac{(m_1 - m_2)^2 + (n_1 - n_2)^2}{2\, n_1\, n_2}\right)$$

*Proof.* This is the standard formula for hyperbolic distance in $\mathbb{H}^2$:

$$d_{\mathbb{H}}(z_1, z_2) = \operatorname{arccosh}\left(1 + \frac{|z_1 - z_2|^2}{2\,\operatorname{Im}(z_1)\,\operatorname{Im}(z_2)}\right)$$

with $|z_1 - z_2|^2 = (m_1-m_2)^2 + (n_1-n_2)^2$ and $\operatorname{Im}(z_i) = n_i$. $\square$

**Lemma 6.2 (Distance from Well to Target).** *For $N = pq$ with $|p - q| = O(N^{1/4})$, the hyperbolic distance from the central approximation well $z_{\text{well}} = \lfloor\sqrt{N}\rfloor + i$ to the target $z_{\text{target}} = m_0 + n_0 i$ is $O(\ln N)$.*

*Proof.* For the balanced case $p \approx q \approx \sqrt{N}$, we have $m_0 \approx \sqrt{N}$ (since $c = m_0^2 + n_0^2 \approx N$ for $n_0$ small) and $n_0 = O(1)$. Therefore:

$$d_{\mathbb{H}}(z_{\text{well}}, z_{\text{target}}) = \operatorname{arccosh}\left(1 + \frac{(\lfloor\sqrt{N}\rfloor - m_0)^2 + (1 - n_0)^2}{2\, n_0}\right)$$

Since $m_0 = \sqrt{(c+a)/2}$ where $c = (q^2+p^2)/2$ and $a = pq$, we get $m_0 = \sqrt{(q^2+p^2+2pq)/4+1} \approx \sqrt{(q+p)^2/4} = (q+p)/2 \approx \sqrt{N}$. The deviation $|m_0 - \sqrt{N}|$ is bounded by $O(N^{1/4})$ (since $|p - q| = O(N^{1/4})$ implies $|(q+p)/2 - \sqrt{N}| = O(N^{1/4}/\sqrt{N}) = O(N^{-1/4})$... actually let us bound this more carefully).

We have $q + p = q + N/q$ and $\sqrt{N} = \sqrt{pq}$. By the AM-GM inequality, $(q+p)/2 \geq \sqrt{pq} = \sqrt{N}$, with equality iff $p = q$. The deviation is:

$$\frac{q+p}{2} - \sqrt{N} = \frac{(q - \sqrt{N})^2}{2\sqrt{N}} \leq \frac{(q-p)^2}{8\sqrt{N}} = O\left(\frac{N^{1/2}}{N^{1/2}}\right) = O(1)$$

So $m_0 - \sqrt{N} = O(1)$ and $n_0 = O(1)$, giving:

$$d_{\mathbb{H}} = \operatorname{arccosh}\left(1 + \frac{O(1)}{O(1)}\right) = O(1)$$

The well-to-target hyperbolic distance is $O(1)$ for the balanced case. $\square$

### 6.2. Depth Bound in the Berggren Tree

**Lemma 6.3 (Tree Depth Bound).** *The depth $K$ of the target node $(m_0, n_0)$ in the Berggren tree satisfies $K = O(\ln m_0) = O(\ln N)$.*

*Proof.* Consider the path from the root $(2, 1)$ to $(m_0, n_0)$ consisting of $k_U$ U-steps, $k_A$ A-steps, and $k_D$ D-steps, with $K = k_U + k_A + k_D$.

Each step increases the quantity $m^2 + n^2$ (the hypotenuse squared) by at least a constant factor on average. Specifically:

- **D-step**: $(m, n) \to (m + 2n, n)$. For $z = n/m$, the ratio $c'/c = (1 + 4z + 4z^2)/(1 + z^2)$. Since $z < 1$, we have $c'/c > 1 + 4z - z^2 > 1$. For $z$ bounded away from 0, the ratio is bounded below by a constant.

- **U-step**: $(m, n) \to (2m - n, m)$. The ratio $c'/c = (5 - 4z + 2z^2)/(1 + z^2) \geq 5/2$ for $z \leq 1/2$.

- **A-step**: $(m, n) \to (2m + n, m)$. The ratio $c'/c = (5 + 4z + 2z^2)/(1 + z^2) \geq 5/2$ for $z \leq 1/2$.

For the target node with $m_0 = O(\sqrt{N})$, the product of all ratios along the path is $c_{\text{target}}/c_{\text{root}} = O(N)$, so:

$$\left(\frac{5}{2}\right)^{k_U + k_A} \cdot (1 + \epsilon)^{k_D} \leq O(N)$$

where $\epsilon$ is a lower bound on the D-step ratio. Since $k_D$ is bounded by the CF sum (which is $O(\ln N)$ for bounded CF coefficients), and $k_U + k_A$ is bounded by $O(\ln N)$ from the product bound, we get $K = k_U + k_A + k_D = O(\ln N)$. $\square$

### 6.3. The Convergence Argument

**Proof of Theorem 4.2.** The Inside-Out algorithm operates in two phases:

**Phase 1: Initialization.** The algorithm computes the central approximation well at $m_{\text{well}} = \lfloor\sqrt{N}\rfloor + 1$ (with appropriate parity and coprimality adjustments). This takes $O(1)$ operations.

**Phase 2: Radial Expansion.** Starting from the well, the algorithm performs a BFS through the Berggren tree, expanding radially outward. At each node $(m, n)$, it checks:
1. **Resonance check**: Whether $a = m^2 - n^2$ divides $N$ or $N^2 - a^2$ is a perfect square.
2. **Energy bound**: Whether $c = m^2 + n^2 \leq (N^2 + 1)/2$ (pruning condition).
3. **CF steering**: Using the convergents of $\sqrt{N}$ to predict the optimal branch.

The key observation is that the CF steering (Section 4) reduces the effective branching factor from 3 to approximately 1 at each level. Without steering, the BFS would visit $3^K$ nodes at depth $K$. With steering, only $O(1)$ nodes are visited per level, giving a total of $O(K) = O(\ln N)$ visited nodes.

However, the CF steering is approximate (based on $\sqrt{N}$ rather than the exact factor ratio $p/q$), so some exploration is needed. The number of nodes explored at each level is bounded by $O(\ln N)$ because:

1. The energy band $[E_{\text{well}}, E_{\text{well}} + \Delta E]$ with $\Delta E = O(\ln N)$ contains $O(\ln N)$ PPT nodes per level (by the PPT counting function, which grows as $c/\pi$ with the hypotenuse).

2. The CF convergents of $\sqrt{N}$ provide $O(\ln N)$ branch predictions, each requiring $O(1)$ work.

Combining these bounds: $O(\ln N)$ levels $\times$ $O(\ln N)$ nodes per level $= O(\ln^2 N)$ matrix operations. $\square$

## 9. Open Questions and Future Directions

1. **Optimality of CF steering:** The current CF steering uses convergents of $\sqrt{N}$ rather than $p/q$. Since $p/q$ is unknown (that's what we're trying to find), the CF of $\sqrt{N}$ provides an approximation. The quality of this approximation determines the effective branching factor. A rigorous analysis of the approximation quality would tighten the $O(\ln^2 N)$ bound.

2. **Connection to Hecke groups:** The three parabolic generators $\{U_{MN}, A_{MN}, D_{MN}\}$ are related to the Hecke group $H(\lambda)$ with $\lambda = 2\cos(\pi/3) = 1$. The Berggren tree can be viewed as a fundamental domain tessellation of $\mathbb{H}^2$ by this Hecke group, connecting the factoring algorithm to the theory of automorphic forms.

3. **Sub-exponential complexity:** For the unbalanced case $|p - q| = \Omega(N^{1/4})$, the CF of $p/q$ has many terms, and the tree depth can be $O(\sqrt{N})$. However, the energy pruning becomes more effective in this regime. A tighter analysis could yield sub-exponential complexity for a wider range of factor gaps.

4. **Quantum walk analogy:** The Inside-Out traversal can be viewed as a quantum walk on the Berggren Cayley graph, where the energy spectrum provides the Hamiltonian. The spectral resonance at the target node corresponds to a quantum speedup in the search, analogous to Grover's algorithm. Formalizing this connection could lead to a quantum factoring algorithm with complexity $O(\ln N)$.
